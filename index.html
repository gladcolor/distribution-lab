What is a distribution?

You dip a ladle into a pond and count the larvae. You dip again one step away and the count is different. What can a pile of unequal counts tell you?

One dip tells you little. The pattern across many dips tells you a lot. A distribution is that pattern: every count that can turn up, with how often each one does.

Statistics keeps a short catalogue of named distributions. Each one comes from a simple story about how the numbers arise. Learn the stories and you can often say which distribution fits your data before you plot it.

Three things to read from any distribution

  • Center. Where the typical value sits: the mean or the median.
  • Spread. How far values stray from the center: the variance and its square root, the standard deviation.
  • Shape. Symmetric or lopsided, how many zeros, how long the tail.

Insect counts are almost never symmetric. Most dips catch few, a handful catch many, and the mean alone hides that. In the lessons below, shape matters as much as the mean.

You take 20 dips and the bars look ragged. What should you conclude?

Lesson 1 · yes or no

Present or absent: the binomial

A habitat gets 20 dips. How many of them will have larvae in them?

Each dip is a yes-or-no trial. If every dip has the same chance p of holding larvae, and the dips do not influence each other, the number of positive dips out of n follows the binomial distribution.

  • The peak sits near n × p. Move p and the whole hill slides.
  • Near p = 50% the hill is symmetric. Near 0% or 100% it leans against the wall, because a count cannot go below zero or above n.
  • The spread is widest at 50% and shrinks toward both ends.

Negative, or just not found yet?

If one dip finds larvae with chance p, then n dips all miss with chance (1 − p)n. A habitat is never proven negative. It is negative at a stated effort.

In the field. Low-density habitats need many more dips than crowded ones. When a protocol fixes the number of dips, this curve tells you which habitats it will quietly miss.

Formulas

Chance of exactly k positives: P(k) = C(n, k) pk (1 − p)n − k

Mean n p, variance n p (1 − p).

Chance of at least one positive: 1 − (1 − p)n. Dips needed for a target: n ≥ ln(1 − target) / ln(1 − p).

One dip finds larvae 10% of the time. About how many dips give a 95% chance of at least one positive dip?

Lesson 2 · counts at random

Scattered at random: the Poisson

If larvae were spread over the water with no pattern at all, what would the counts per dip look like?

When individuals land independently of each other, and each dip samples a small part of the whole, the count per dip follows the Poisson distribution. It has a single dial, the mean. Its signature: the variance equals the mean.

  • Keep clumping at none and press Scatter again a few times. The bars hug the dashed Poisson line, and the variance to mean ratio hovers near 1.
  • Even a random scatter has empty squares and crowded squares. Random does not mean even.
  • Now raise the clumping. The mean stays where it was, but empty dips and very full dips both multiply, and the Poisson line no longer fits. That is the next lesson.

In the field. The variance to mean ratio is one of the oldest tools in insect ecology. Near 1 suggests a random pattern, well above 1 a clumped one, and below 1 an even one, as with territorial animals. Check it before you choose a model for counts.

Formulas

Chance of exactly k: P(k) = λk e−λ / k!, where λ is the mean.

Mean λ, variance λ. Chance of an empty dip: e−λ.

Counts per trap have a mean of 4 and a variance of 21. Is a Poisson model reasonable?

Lesson 3 · clumped counts

Clumped counts: the negative binomial

Real larvae are clumped. Most dips are nearly empty and a few are crowded. Which distribution describes that?

Suppose every spot has its own Poisson mean: high along a warm, shallow edge with food, low in open water. Mix those Poisson counts together and you get the negative binomial. It has two dials: the mean m and the aggregation parameter k. Small k means strong clumping. As k grows the clumping fades, and at very large k the negative binomial turns into the Poisson.

  • Slide k down toward 0.2. The zero bar grows and the tail stretches to the right, while the mean does not move.
  • Slide k up past 10. The orange and green curves merge.
  • The variance is m + m²/k. The second term is the extra spread that clumping adds.

Why would counts clump? Eggs are laid in batches, larvae gather along edges and in shade, and neighboring habitats differ in food and predators. Each of these makes the local mean change from place to place.

In the field. Clumping is expensive. The last tile shows how many dips you need before the standard error of the mean falls to about 20% of the mean. With k below 1 the answer is driven almost entirely by k: a pond with more larvae is not easier to measure.

Formulas, and Taylor's power law

Mean m, variance m + m² / k. Chance of an empty dip: (1 + m / k)−k.

Dips needed for a standard error that is a fraction D of the mean: n = (1 / m + 1 / k) / D².

Across many sites, the variance tends to rise with the mean as a power law, s² = a mb, with b usually between 1 (random) and 2 (clumping that grows in step with the mean). This is Taylor's power law.

Two ponds have the same mean of 5 larvae per dip. Pond A has k = 0.5 and pond B has k = 8. Which statement is right?

Lesson 4 · counts with many zeros

Too many zeros: zero-inflated counts

Half the habitats in a survey have no larvae. Bad luck, or a different kind of habitat?

Two processes can hide behind one column of data. First: can this habitat hold larvae at all? A pit that dried last week, a polluted drain or a covered tank cannot. Second: if it can, how many did the dips catch? A zero-inflated model keeps the two apart. A share of habitats always gives zero, and the rest follow a count distribution such as the negative binomial.

  • Raise the unsuitable share. Only the zero bar grows. The shape of the counts above zero does not change.
  • Now set it to 0% and lower k. The zero bar grows again, for a different reason: clumping. From a histogram alone the two are hard to tell apart.
  • Tick the zoom box to look at the counts above zero.

In the field. The difference matters for control. An unsuitable habitat needs no larvicide. A suitable habitat with a zero today may be productive after the next rain. Field notes such as water present, habitat type and shade are what let a model separate the two kinds of zero.

Formulas, and the hurdle model

With a share π of unsuitable habitats: chance of a zero π + (1 − π) (1 + m / k)−k, mean over all habitats (1 − π) m.

A close cousin, the hurdle model, treats all zeros as one group and models the positive counts separately. It answers a slightly different question: is anything there, and if so, how much?

The share of unsuitable habitats rises from 20% to 60% and nothing else changes. What happens to the counts in suitable habitats?

Lesson 5 · skewed measurements

Skewed measurements: the lognormal

Larval density, habitat area, development time: positive numbers with a long right tail. How should they be summarized?

Some quantities are built by multiplying many small effects: growth, survival from one stage to the next, dilution. Multiplication skews. Take logarithms and multiplication becomes addition, and sums of many small effects are bell-shaped. A quantity whose logarithm is normal is called lognormal.

  • On the natural scale the mean sits to the right of the median, pulled by the tail. Widen the spread and the gap grows.
  • Switch to the log scale. The bars form a symmetric bell, and on that scale the mean and the median coincide.
  • The last tile shows how much of the total sits in the densest tenth of habitats. With a wide spread, a few habitats dominate.

In the field. For skewed measurements, report the median or the geometric mean and not only the arithmetic mean. For counts with zeros, entomologists often average log(count + 1) and transform back, which is known as Williams' mean. The gamma distribution is a close alternative to the lognormal with a lighter tail.

Formulas

If ln X is normal with mean μ and standard deviation σ: median eμ, mean eμ + σ²/2, coefficient of variation √(eσ² − 1).

Densities in 50 habitats follow a lognormal distribution. Which is larger?

Lesson 6 · time until an event

Lifetimes: survival and the exponential

If an adult mosquito survives each day with the same chance, how many live long enough to pass on malaria?

With a constant daily survival p, the share of a cohort still alive after t days is pt. Counted in whole days this is the geometric distribution. In continuous time it is the exponential. It has no memory: a mosquito that has survived ten days faces the same risk tomorrow as one that emerged today.

  • The malaria parasite needs roughly 10 days or more inside the mosquito before it can be passed on, depending on the parasite species and the temperature. Only mosquitoes that reach the shaded zone can transmit.
  • Drop daily survival from 90% to 80%. The share reaching day 10 falls from 35% to 11%.
  • The last tile, infective days per emerging mosquito, falls faster still.

In the field. This steep response is the classic argument for tools that shorten adult life, such as treated nets and indoor spraying. Larval source management pulls a different lever: it lowers the number of adults that emerge in the first place. Real mosquitoes also age, so survival is not exactly constant. The Weibull and Gompertz distributions add that.

Formulas

Share alive after t days: pt. Average life: 1 / (−ln p) days.

Expected infective life per emerging mosquito, with an incubation period of n days: pn / (−ln p).

Daily survival is a constant 85%. A mosquito has already lived 12 days. What is its chance of surviving tomorrow?

Lesson 7 · proportions

Uncertain proportions: the beta

3 of 20 habitats were positive. What might the true share be across the whole town?

A proportion lives between 0 and 1, and the beta distribution is the standard curve on that interval. Start with no preference for any value, observe x positives out of n, and the plausible values of the true share follow a beta distribution with parameters x + 1 and n − x + 1.

  • With 3 of 20 the observed share is 15%, but true shares from about 5% to 36% remain plausible.
  • Set 30 of 200, the same 15%. The range narrows to about 11% to 21%.
  • Set 0 of 20. The observed share is zero, yet true shares up to about 16% are still plausible. Finding nothing is not the same as nothing being there.

In the field. To halve the width of the range you need about four times as many habitats. Near 0% or 100% the curve is lopsided, so a symmetric plus-or-minus interval would be wrong there.

Formulas

Beta(a, b) has mean a / (a + b). After x positives in n habitats, starting from a flat curve: Beta(x + 1, n − x + 1).

No positive habitat was found among 20 checked. What can you say about the town?

Lesson 8 · averages

Averages behave: the normal distribution

Single counts are wildly skewed. Why do averages over many habitats look bell-shaped?

Add up many independent values, whatever their shape, and the sum drifts toward the normal distribution. This is the central limit theorem, and it is the reason the bell curve turns up in so many formulas. The board of pegs at the top of this page shows the same thing: every bead takes ten random steps left or right, and the piles form a bell.

  • Set the first slider to 1 habitat. The right chart is as skewed as the left.
  • At 10 habitats the averages are still lopsided. By 30 they are close to the bell, and at 100 the fit is tight.
  • The spread of the averages is the spread of single counts divided by the square root of n. Four times the fieldwork halves the error.
  • Lower k. With stronger clumping the bell takes longer to arrive.

In the field. The standard error, and the familiar interval of plus or minus two standard errors, both rest on this theorem. They can be trusted for averages over many habitats. They are shaky for small, heavily clumped samples.

Formulas

For n independent values with mean μ and standard deviation σ, the average is close to normal with mean μ and standard deviation σ / √n, the standard error.

To halve the standard error of a mean density, how many times more habitats must you sample?

Lesson 9 · when no formula fits

Distributions you build yourself: bootstrap and permutation

A model ranks habitats, and the top-ranked half turns out to hold most of the larvae. How precise is that number, and could it be luck?

Some statistics have no textbook formula. Then you let the computer build the distribution from the data.

The bootstrap asks how far the number would move in another survey. It draws the habitats again with replacement, thousands of times, and looks at the spread. The permutation test asks what chance alone would do. It shuffles the ranking so that it carries no information, thousands of times, and sees how often the shuffled result matches the real one.

  • Bootstrap view. The shaded band is the middle 95% of the resampled values. It is the interval you would report.
  • Permutation view. The bars now pile up around 50%, which is what a ranking with no skill achieves. The P value is the share of shuffles that did at least as well as your survey.
  • Set skill to none and press New survey a few times. The observed value lands anywhere in the pile and the P value is unremarkable, as it should be.
  • With only 60 habitats and clumped counts, the bootstrap interval is wide even for a good ranking.

In the field. Both tools are routine when a ranking model is evaluated: a bootstrap interval around the score, and a permutation test against random ordering. One rule matters more than any formula: resample the unit you sampled. If habitats were surveyed, resample habitats, not single dips.

A permutation test gives P = 0.40. What does that mean?

Lesson 10 · putting it to work

Why skew is good news for targeted control

If counts are clumped and many habitats are empty, where are the larvae, and how much can targeting gain?

Every lesson so far said the same thing in a different way: larval counts are uneven. Put the habitats in order from most to least productive, add up the larvae as you go, and the unevenness becomes a curve. If larvae were spread evenly the curve would follow the diagonal. The more clumped the counts, the higher it bulges.

  • Slide Habitats you can treat and read how much of the larvae they hold. Compare it with the same effort spent at random.
  • Raise the share of empty habitats, or lower k, and the bulge grows.
  • The orange curve is a ceiling. It uses the counts themselves to choose the order, which no planner can do in advance. Random order follows the diagonal. A real ranking model lands in between, and the fraction of that gap it closes is its score. Some projects call that fraction the lift-ratio. Elsewhere the same number is known as the accuracy ratio or the normalized Gini coefficient.

In the field. A single survey flatters the ceiling. Counts are noisy and habitats change with rain and season, so the habitats on top today will not all be on top next month. Skew tells you that targeting can pay. It does not tell you that you can find the targets.

In a town where every habitat held the same number of larvae, how much would a perfect ranking gain over random order?

Words used on this page

Mean
The sum of the values divided by how many there are.
Median
The middle value: half are below it and half above.
Variance
The average squared distance from the mean. Its square root is the standard deviation.
Standard error
The standard deviation of an estimate, such as a mean, across repeated surveys.
Parameter
A dial that sets a distribution's center, spread or shape, such as the mean or k.
Overdispersion
More spread than the Poisson allows: a variance larger than the mean.
Aggregation parameter k
The dial of the negative binomial that controls clumping. Small means clumped.
Structural zero
A zero that had to happen, because the habitat cannot hold larvae.
Quantile
The value below which a given share of the data falls. The median is the 50% quantile.
Interval
A range of plausible values for an unknown quantity, at a stated level such as 95%.
P value
How often chance alone would give a result at least as extreme as the one observed.
